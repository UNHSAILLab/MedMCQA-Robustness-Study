Finding 1 · Chain-of-thought

Watch reasoning talk itself out of the right answer

Finding 2 · Position bias

Few-shot examples collapse the model onto option A

Compare where the correct answers actually sit against where the model puts its predictions. Add few-shot examples and the model answers "A" three times out of four, regardless of content.

Finding 3 · Option order

Does the model read the question, or the position?

Take one real anatomy question. Keep the words identical and only move the options around. The model keeps picking the same letter, which means it follows the slot on the screen, not the content in it.

Finding 4 · Context truncation

How you cut the context matters more than how much

Feed the model an abstract, then cut it different ways. Keeping the opening is nearly free. Removing the opening and keeping only the tail drops accuracy below answering with no context at all.

Finding 5 · Reading the log-probs

The model knows more than its generated text shows

The bigger model looks worse when it has to write an answer. Read its option probabilities instead of its generated text, and it jumps to the best score in the whole study. Same model, same questions, different readout.

When Chain-of-Thought Backfires

Standard prompt-engineering best practices do not transfer to domain-specific medical language models. Reliable alternatives, including cloze scoring, permutation voting, and end-truncation, recover most of what prompting loses.
